## How to Protect the Name

### 1. Trademark Registration (Primary Tool)

A **trademark** protects a name, logo, or slogan used in commerce. It is the main legal tool for protecting a game title.

**Where to file:**
| Territory | Body | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| United States | USPTO (teas.uspto.gov) | Required for US distribution |
| European Union | EUIPO (euipo.europa.eu) | Covers all EU member states |
| International | WIPO Madrid Protocol | Bundle filing for many countries; higher cost |

**Relevant classes for a video game:**
- **Class 9** — downloadable software / video games
- **Class 41** — entertainment services

**Typical cost through an attorney:** $1,500–$3,000 for a US filing including search and legal fees. International filings add cost per territory.

**Timeline:** USPTO review takes 8–12 months on average. You can use ™ immediately on filing; ® only after registration is granted.

---

### 2. The Prior Art Risk — Act on This First

Before filing anything, a **clearance search** must be done on:

- **"BoxForged"** — the full name
- **"BoxHead"** — the primary distinctive term

There is a known prior use: the *Boxhead* flash game series published by Crazy Monkey Games around 2006. Flash games are largely defunct, but prior commercial use — even abandoned — can complicate or block a trademark application depending on whether the mark was formally maintained.

**A trademark attorney will determine:**
- Whether the *Boxhead* mark was ever registered
- Whether it has been abandoned (non-use for 3+ years creates vulnerability)
- Whether our use is sufficiently distinct (different genre, different style, "Ninjas" suffix)
- Whether to proceed with the name as-is, modify it, or choose a different name

**Do not launch the game publicly under this name until the clearance search is done.**

---

### 3. Copyright (Already Active)

Copyright protects the creative work itself — code, art, music, the Game Design Document, character designs. It applies automatically at the moment of creation and requires no filing.

Copyright does **not** protect the game's name or title. That is trademark's job.

**What this means for us now:** Our code and design documents are already protected. No action needed for copyright. Focus effort on the trademark question.

---

### 4. Common Law Trademark

Using a name in commerce (selling the game, publishing it publicly) creates limited **common law trademark** rights in the geographic area where you operate — even without a federal registration. This is weaker than a registered trademark and harder to enforce, but it exists.

**Practical implication:** The longer we operate publicly under the name, the stronger our common law claim — but a registered mark from another party can still override it. Registration is worth pursuing.
