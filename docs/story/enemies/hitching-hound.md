# Hitching Hound

**Zone:** The Cul-de-Sac

**What it actually is:** A hitching post. The decorative kind that sat at the end of someone's driveway — cast-iron, painted black, shaped like a small jockey figure with a ring through its outstretched hand. Common enough in this neighborhood that nobody thought about them. The Unimaginative tagged the ring as a tethering asset, left the post in place.

The post kept the ring. The ring swings. The figure remembers standing at the edge of a driveway for twenty years, waiting, holding a ring no horse ever used. The waiting became something.

**What the box sees:** A low, heavy brawler — iron-dark, compact, built for holding on. The ring is still in its hand. It uses the ring like a flail: short arcs, close quarters, punishing. It does not move fast. It doesn't have to. It moves like something that has learned patience by being tethered to a spot for two decades, and is now finding out what it can do with four feet of clearance in every direction.

---

## Lore

It was never a real hound. That was always a figure of speech — hitching post, hitching hound, the thing you tied your horse to before going inside. The neighborhood hadn't seen a horse in living memory. The post was irony that calcified into furniture. It stood at the driveway's edge and was ignored so completely that it became invisible. The Unimaginative walked past it without tagging it. It did not appear on the manifest.

The imagination does not need to be on a manifest to find something.

---

## Combat Role

**Type:** Skirmisher / melee brawler. Chains attacks in close quarters: two ring-flail swings, then a shoulder check, then another swing. The sequence is consistent. The player who learns the sequence can parry into it; the player who doesn't gets driven backward into a wall.

**Special — Tether Rush:** Once per encounter, the Hitching Hound uses the ring to grab Kid and pull her toward it. Not a grapple in the rope sense — it swings the ring like a lasso, hooks something on Kid's clothing or box, and yanks. The pull is short but removes Kid's positioning entirely. She ends up in the Hound's preferred range: close enough that the ring-flail is unblockable unless she dodges through it.

Counter: the grapple rope. If Kid has the rope and hooks an anchor point before the Tether Rush connects, she maintains tension from two directions. The Hound cannot complete the pull. The stalemate lasts one second — long enough to dodge the follow-through.

---

## Behavioral Notes (for animators / designers)

The HitchingHound paces a short chain radius, always taut — it wants to chase but something in its body memory knows the boundary. When the player approaches, it lunges as far as it can reach, then pulls itself back. Frustrated. Leashed. This behavior reads as aggressive but is closer to anxious: it has spent twenty years at the edge of a driveway, and twenty years of standing still does not leave a body gracefully.

The pacing should be small — three steps in one direction, three in the other, at the edge of the imagined tether. The lunge: committed, full body, the ring swinging wide. The snap-back: not a retreat but a return, the way something that was tied to a post returns to the post even after the rope is gone.

When the tether is cut — when Kid defeats it — the Hitching Hound takes three full steps away from the spot where it stood. Just three. Then it stops. It has never walked further than that. It doesn't know what's out there. It sits.

---

## Audio

- Idle pacing: a low iron-on-stone drag, rhythmic
- Ring swing: a weight-on-chain sound — controlled, not wild
- Shoulder check: a flat iron-against-surface impact
- Tether Rush: the ring spinning before the hook, then a sharp clank of contact
- Defeat: the sound of something settling that has never had the chance to settle before

---

## Sample Lines

The Hitching Hound does not speak. It has a ring and a radius and patience built up over two decades of standing at the end of a driveway. That is enough.

---

## What Its Presence Means

A Hitching Hound in a room means the zone has been held long enough that the environmental objects have had time to develop character. The Wagon Wheel Roller arrives in the early rooms — fast, improvised, before the Unimaginative's systems have fully settled. The Hitching Hound arrives later: something that has been standing at its post since before any of this started. It is not newly dangerous. It has been waiting.

The fight is not about beating something sent to stop you. It is about dealing with something that was already there, waiting for exactly this, for twenty years.
