# The Great Hush — Foundational Story Content

---

## 1. World Bible Entry — The Great Hush

Nobody announced it.

That is the thing people always mention first, when they mention it at all. There was no warning. No press release. No countdown. One morning the connection was simply gone — not slow, not intermittent, the way it had been during storms or outages. Gone. Like a sound you hadn't noticed until the room went silent and you realized it had been underneath everything, all along.

The people who did it — engineers, mostly. The kind whose names appeared on no websites because they were too busy keeping the websites running. Infrastructure people. The ones who knew where the actual wires went. They had spent years watching something happen that they could not explain to anyone who hadn't already seen it. Neighbors who shared a wall and had never spoken. Children who sat in the same room as their parents and were somewhere else entirely. Whole communities of people who had outsourced the act of being present to a device that fit in a pocket.

They talked about it among themselves, for a long time. Then they stopped talking and did something.

Immediately after: a strange quiet. Not peaceful. The kind of quiet that follows a loud noise, when your ears are still ringing and you're not sure if you're frightened or relieved. Adults stood in yards, in parking lots, in kitchens, holding phones that had become rectangles of glass. Some of them cried. Some of them looked at the sky, which was the same sky it had always been, and seemed to be noticing it for the first time.

The kids who had never had phones didn't understand what had been lost. They went outside. Some of them found cardboard boxes. Some of them already knew what to do with a cardboard box.

The world now is a quieter world. Some neighborhoods never came back — the grey settled in and stayed, and the people in them walk slowly, like they're waiting for something to load. In other places, color returned. You can usually tell by whether the children are outside.

Whether the Elders were right is not a question the world has answered yet. Maybe the world never will. What they did is the weight behind a phrase you'll hear if you're somewhere adults forget themselves for a moment. They say it quietly. They don't elaborate.

They just say: *The Great Hush.* And then they're quiet again, which is its own kind of answer.

## 2. Opening Prologue

*Quiet. Then a voice — an adult's, somewhere between tired and careful.*

---

The Internet didn't break.

Someone turned it off. People who understood exactly what they were holding — the whole weight of how everyone had learned to talk, to work, to remember, to love — and they let go of it anyway.

They thought it was the right thing to do.

Maybe it was.

The world after was quieter. Some people never got their color back. They walk around grey, now, looking for something to tell them what to feel.

But some kids — the ones who had already been going outside, already been picking up sticks and calling them swords, already been turning cardboard into helmets and crowns and the cockpits of spacecraft —

those kids didn't miss a step.

One of them found a box in the recycling.

He looked at it for a moment.

Then he put it on his head.

*A beat. Color floods into the frame.*

And the world became what it had always been, underneath.

## 3. The Moment the Kid Hears It

*Interior — a neighbor's porch. Late afternoon. Kid is cutting through the side yard on his way somewhere more important. Two adults sit in lawn chairs, facing away from him. They have drinks. They're not arguing, not celebrating. They're just talking the way adults talk when the day is almost over.*

---

**NEIGHBOR:** We're going to sell. Probably in the spring.

**MOM:** I thought you loved that house.

**NEIGHBOR:** We did.

*A pause. Ice shifts in a glass.*

**NEIGHBOR:** It's just — after the Hush, Dave never really... he was one of those people who needed the noise, you know? And then there wasn't any. And he just.

*She doesn't finish the sentence. She doesn't have to.*

**MOM:** Yeah.

*Another pause. Longer.*

**NEIGHBOR:** You hear about the Garcias? On the next block?

**MOM:** With the daughter?

**NEIGHBOR:** Mm. She's doing better. Found some other kids to — I don't know, they do whatever kids do now. Run around.

*She says it like she's describing something from another century. Not unkindly. Just distant.*

**NEIGHBOR:** I think that's actually good for her.

**MOM:** I think so too.

*Kid has stopped moving. He's behind the fence, in the gap between the two yards. He can see the tops of their heads. He didn't mean to hear any of this and he's not sure he was supposed to.*

*He doesn't know what the Hush is. He knows it's heavy. He knows it's the reason things are the way they are.*

*He looks at the cardboard box he's been carrying under his arm.*

*He keeps walking. He doesn't say anything. He's already thinking.*

## 4. Ambient Adult Whisper Lines

*Environmental audio — things adults say when they think no one young is listening. These play as Kid moves through zones near adult figures. Flat delivery. No music underneath.*

---

1. "I just keep thinking — if it had happened a year later, or a year earlier — do you think we would have been different people by then?"

2. "She asked me why I was sad and I didn't know how to start. I just said 'grown-up stuff.' Which is the worst thing you can say."

3. "The Hush wasn't the end of anything. It was more like — a reveal. You find out who people actually are when there's nothing to look at."

4. "I'm not angry at them. I think I was, for a while. Now I just think they were as scared as we were."

5. "Dave still has all his passwords written down in a notebook. Just in case. I don't have the heart to tell him."

6. "She was born after it. She doesn't have anything to compare it to. Sometimes I think that's the lucky thing."

7. "I keep forgetting I can't just look it up. Five years. I keep forgetting."

8. "You know what the weird part is? The weird part is the first week was the hardest. After that, it was just — Tuesday."

9. "They knew what they were doing. I've made peace with that. Knowing what you're doing and being right are two different things."

10. "He never told anyone. Not a single name. I used to think that was cowardly. I think now it was something else."

## 5. Name Development

The inciting event went through several candidate names before landing on The Great Hush.

| Name | Register | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **The Great Hush** *(confirmed)* | Historical grief | Weight of a folktale; adults whisper it; self-referential when spoken quietly. |
| The Hush | Whispered grief | Runner-up; intimate, self-referential, childlike. |
| The Break | Universal loss | Things broke. People broke. Relationships broke. |
| The Still | Eerie, minimal | Two syllables. "Ever since The Still…" |
| The Gone | Pure grief | "Back before The Gone." Pre-verbal in weight. |
| The Long Quiet | Folktale | Something a grandparent says; implies it hasn't ended. |
| The Silence | Original working name | Clean and ominous; retired in favor of The Great Hush. |

## 6. Story Implications

- The Elders are morally complex, not villains. Their motivation was legitimate. The game does not resolve whether they were right.
- The Elders are engineers — people who understood the infrastructure and had the access. The Great Hush required coordination and technical knowledge, not magic or authority.
- The Unimaginative are victims of the cure, not agents of the disease.
- The Kid is what the Elders were hoping for — someone who never outsourced his imagination in the first place. They just didn't know he existed.
- Imagination in this world is not whimsy. It is the practical skill the Elders were trying to restore, arrived at by a different path than they expected.
- The game is set in the present. The suburban world is familiar. The strangeness is the silence where the screen used to be.
