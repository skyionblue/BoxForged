# The Kid

**She/her/hers throughout all story content. Never he, him, his. Never "the player" as a substitute for a gendered pronoun when referring to Kid's inner life. She is a girl. This is not ambiguous.**

---

## The Cardboard Lens

The box is not what makes her imaginative. The box reveals that she always was.

This distinction matters. Every other character in the world who picks up a box sees a box. Kid picks up a box and it becomes a lens — the thing through which the imagined layer of the world becomes visible. The imagination is hers. The box just lets other people see what she sees.

The lens is literal in the game: she cuts a hole in the box and looks through it, and the backyard is different. Not magically transformed. Just: seen as what it is underneath. Seen correctly.

The box on her head is not a costume. It is not a character gimmick. It is a kid who found a way to show people what she can see, and never took it off because the world has not been reclaimed yet.

## Backstory

Before the box, she was already the kind of kid who made things. Cardboard shields from Amazon deliveries. Rubber-band catapults. Balsa-wood swords that snapped on the first swing, and she'd just make another one. Her parents never bought her a tablet. They never explained why — they'd just redirect her. Outside. Kitchen table. Here's some tape. She resented it, sometimes, the way you resent not getting something everyone else has when you don't yet understand what it cost them.

The day The Great Hush happened, she was in the backyard trying to build a trebuchet from PVC pipe and a plastic bin. She heard a distant noise — not a bang, more like a held breath being released. Then her neighbor stopped mowing. The guy across the street sat down in his driveway. Her mom came outside and looked at her phone for a long time and then put it in her pocket and didn't take it out again.

She didn't fully understand what had happened. She went back to the trebuchet.

Two days later, she found the box. It was in the recycling bin — oversized, brown, the kind that held a small appliance. She picked it up because it was the right size to be a shield. She turned it over. Cut the flap corners with a pocketknife. She looked through the hole, and the backyard looked different through it. Not literally — the same grass, the same fence, the same dead apple tree. But she saw it as something, the way you see a cloud as a rabbit. And then the rabbit started moving.

She ran inside for markers. She's been running somewhere ever since.

## Personality Portrait

The Kid does not perform enthusiasm. She is genuinely, fundamentally inside whatever she's doing. When she fights a Gnome Soldier, she's not pretending it's a battle — it is a battle. When she calls the cul-de-sac a main street, she's not being creative — she's reporting what she sees. The box does not make her imaginative. It reveals that she always was.

She is not precocious. She doesn't have clever things to say about the world. She has conclusions, stated simply, and then she acts on them. She sees the shape of a situation and moves. This can read as fearlessness, but it isn't. It's closer to not having time to be afraid when there's something that needs doing.

She will not be talked out of anything by someone who hasn't tried it. The Skeptic's words land — you can see them land — but they do not stop her. She pauses. She looks at what she's fighting for. She keeps going.

What drives her: she wants the apple tree to have its blossoms back. She doesn't articulate it that way. She wouldn't know how. But that's what she's moving toward. Everything that's gone grey — every flat quiet place that used to be alive — she wants it back. She knows, somehow, that it's still there underneath.

The specific face she is walking toward: Mr. Wen. Her neighbor. A martial arts instructor — had a dojo a few blocks away, ran classes after school for kids on the block. Grew tomatoes. Had a laugh she could hear through her bedroom window on autumn afternoons. He used to sit in the roots of the old tree at the edge of the neighborhood on Sunday mornings. She never asked him about it. She knows now why it matters.

After The Hush, he went grey — not sad, not angry, just not there in the way he used to be there. She has never said his name out loud in the game. He is not a quest marker. He is the reason the whole enterprise matters. The World Tree is not abstract. The World Tree is Mr. Wen's window boxes, empty. The Lens cannot reach him — he is not somewhere imagination can simply fix. She can only leave what she finds in the roots and trust that the cardboard knows the way back.

## Inner Monologue

Things the Kid thinks but doesn't say out loud:

"I know this is the right way. I don't know how I know."

"If I can see it, it's real. Everything else is just people who stopped looking."

"The house on the corner used to have a tire swing. Someone took it down. I'm going to find out where they put it."

"I'm not scared. I'm. I'm — okay, a little scared. But I'm going anyway."
