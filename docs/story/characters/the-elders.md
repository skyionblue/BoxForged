# The Elders

## Canonical Facts — For All Writers

- **Exactly two.** Not a committee, not a movement. Two people.
- **Friends since junior high school.** This is how they knew each other well enough to have the conversation that changed the world. They had been calling each other wrong without losing each other since they were thirteen.
- **Worked multiple jobs together as adults.** Not just in the same industry — the same workplaces, the same overnight maintenance windows, the same document trails. The trust between them was professional as well as personal.
- **No names. No descriptions. No faces.** They are infrastructure people. The kind who kept the websites running while everyone else looked at the websites. Do not name them. Do not describe their appearance. Do not give them dialogue except in the form of documents and fragments written before the Hush.
- **Morally complex — not villains, not heroes.** The game does not resolve whether they were right. Do not resolve it.

---

## Portrait

They were the people who knew where the actual wires went.

Not the visible ones — not the executives or the platform founders whose names appeared in headlines. The Elders were infrastructure people: the kind who kept the websites running while everyone else looked at the websites. They worked the overnight maintenance windows and documented the architecture nobody else read and quietly fixed the outage before anyone noticed there had been one. They understood systems the way surgeons understand bodies: not abstractly, but in the specific language of what breaks and how, and what it costs when you don't fix it.

Both of them had been doing this work for over twenty years. Long enough to watch the thing they'd helped build become something they no longer recognized. They had been friends since junior high — had called each other wrong without losing each other since they were thirteen — and had worked multiple jobs together as adults. The trust between them was professional as well as personal. When the conversation finally shifted from "what has this become" to "what would happen if," there was only one person each of them would have it with.

They watched attention spans flatten to nothing. They watched the infrastructure they'd maintained get repurposed, at a scale that was genuinely astonishing, for delivering rage and loneliness and advertising in a loop. They watched people stop knowing how to fix things — not just systems, but pipes, relationships, neighborhood trust, the time it takes to build something with your hands and know it's solid. They watched kids stop going outside. They watched their own kids stop going outside. They pulled twelve-hour shifts keeping the machinery running, went home, and watched their families stare into the thing they'd spent their lives maintaining.

The conversation was slow. Encrypted. Professional. They were careful people. They didn't move fast. They thought in systems and edge cases and unintended consequences.

They ran the thought experiment. They ran it again. They built models. They stress-tested them. They were not ideologues. They were engineers. They wanted to see the math first.

The math, eventually, said: survivable. Hard. Not clean. But survivable.

They set a date. They did the work. They didn't announce themselves.

Neither of them believed they were definitely right. That's the weight they carry. Engineers don't believe in certainty — they believe in load-bearing calculations and acceptable margins of error. They calculated that the margin was acceptable. They understood that acceptable is not the same as good.

## The Moral Weight

They did not do this for power. There was no power in what they did — they erased themselves along with everything else. They did it because they had watched the alternative run for twenty years and they knew what the trend lines said.

This does not make them right. It does not make them wrong. It makes them people who made a catastrophic decision in the sincere belief that the catastrophe they were choosing was smaller than the catastrophe they were preventing.

The kids — including Kid — are the evidence one way or another. The Elders don't know how the experiment turns out. They are still alive, somewhere, watching. Trying to rebuild something useful with their hands. Waiting to find out if the math was right.

## Fragments

Things one of them might have written in a private document the night before The Great Hush:

*"We have been maintaining a system that no longer serves the function it was designed to serve. This is not a metaphor. This is a load analysis."*

*"I keep thinking about my daughter's hands. She used to know what to do with them. I don't know when that stopped. I need to know when that stopped."*

*"If we are wrong — and we may be wrong — what we are wrong about is the recovery time. Not the diagnosis. I am certain of the diagnosis."*

*"I don't want credit for this. I want the apple trees to come back. I don't know if they will. I don't know if I'll be alive to see it. I'm doing it anyway."*
